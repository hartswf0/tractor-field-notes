Operative Ekphrasis: WAG Protocols for Semantic-to-Spatial Compilation

Watson Hartsoe

Georgia Institute of Technology, PhD Candidate, Digital Media
December 2024

Abstract. Spatial world-building remains bottlenecked by engine fluency (scene graphs, transforms, scripting), which functions as an implicit permission structure selecting for tooling literacy over domain expertise. This paper presents Words Assemble Geometry (WAG), a pipeline treating natural language as executable spatial specification. Extending operative ekphrasis from text-to-image to text-to-world, WAG routes prompts through planning, compilation into inspectable world files, and browser rendering. The central challenge is semantic fidelity, preserving meaning through compilation, not geometric fidelity. Findings from participatory work with co-designers show that constraints and legible representations enable rapid iteration without semantic collapse.

1. Introduction: Language as Leverage Point

Dominant 3D toolchains require fluency in engineering abstractions rather than domain concepts. This creates a mismatch: people most qualified to author meaningful spaces lack translation infrastructure, not vision. WAG reframes this as a problem of permission, asking who can author spatial worlds in durable, revisable ways.

The intervention is linguistic. Language becomes a leverage point: by treating prompts as executable specifications rather than descriptive hints, authorship shifts from manipulating engine primitives to expressing cultural, narrative, and spatial intent. Meaning becomes input; compilation becomes infrastructure.

2. Thick Prompting and Operative Ekphrasis

Operative ekphrasis describes text that produces images through computation. WAG extends this to three-dimensional space: prompts become constraints compiled into navigable artifacts. But effective prompts are not simple text strings. A single prompt does not permit control and tends to produce results at the average default style of the generator.

Prompts must be thick: densely layered, context-rich instructions carrying embedded cultural weight. The term deliberately recalls Geertz's "thick description." Cultural practices cannot be reduced to simple formulae, and neither can spatial intent. Thick prompts transform human-AI collaboration from mechanical query-response into genuine creative dialogue.

In practice, prompts are multimodal and heterogeneous: combinations of natural language, structured tags, markup languages like POML (Portable Object Markup Language), and JSON. They may look code-like but function as texts for operative ekphrasis, not code to be executed, but specifications to be compiled into geometry.

3. The WAG Pipeline

Intent → Planning (constraints) → Compilation (world file) → Render → Iterate

WAG is built on the LEGOS ontology, routing prompts through a geometric language called LDraw, a thirty-year-old plain-text format for LEGO geometry. Scenes are described in human-readable coordinates: brick positions, orientations, colors. This representation is compiled into a 3D viewport, then fed into image generators (DALL-E, MidJourney), iterated, and optionally synthesized into video (Luma Labs Ray3).

The short film Carrot Uprising, "bureaucracy transformed into garden through collective joy," demonstrates this workflow: geometry seeds the image, image seeds the video. The prompting process is a performative dialogue across multiple models. At each stage, the author revises meaning ("make the threshold quieter") rather than geometry directly.

4. Inspectable Representations

Infrastructure becomes visible upon breakdown. WAG designs for visibility before failure: world files are diffable, auditable, shareable. The LDraw precedent demonstrates the value of human-readable spatial scripts and versionable artifacts. A world is a file and a history, not just a viewport. When compilation is legible, breakdowns can be diagnosed, making prompting craft rather than luck.

5. Findings

Participatory work with co-designers, Jordan Caldwell (Afrofuturist world-building) and Terhi Marttila (digital literature), yielded three patterns:

F1: Permission becomes iterative capability. Fast prompt-to-world loops convert authorship from rare events to ongoing practice.

F2: Semantic fidelity dominates geometric fidelity. Failures were about meaning-loss, cultural protocols becoming tropes, poetic structure becoming literal dressing, not polygon quality.

F3: Constraints enable wildness. Layout rules, affordances, and narrative sequence bound the design space so compilation can guess freely within meaningful limits.

6. Conclusion

WAG supports a pluriversal stance: many authored worlds with different constraints and practices, not convergence into a single platform ontology. The key move is infrastructural. Make compilation legible, constrain failure space, keep representations durable. Language, treated as leverage point, opens spatial authorship to those whose expertise is cultural, narrative, or poetic rather than technical.
