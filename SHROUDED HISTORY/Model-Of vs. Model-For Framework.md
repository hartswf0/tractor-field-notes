# **Encoding History, Decoding Trauma: A Computational and Curatorial Analysis of the O'Hanlon-Olivier Complex at the University of Kentucky**

## **1\. Introduction: The Custodial Paradox and the "God Prompt"**

The University of Kentucky’s Memorial Hall houses a collision of two distinct epistemological frameworks, manifested in physical space as two artworks separated by eighty-four years yet inextricably linked by the custodial crisis of the twenty-first century. On the walls of the lobby exists Ann Rice O’Hanlon’s *History of Kentucky* (1934), a fresco commissioned by the Public Works of Art Project (PWAP) that functions as a "Model Of" the state’s history—a descriptive, representational archive of the 1930s social imaginary. In the vestibule immediately preceding it exists Karyn Olivier’s *Witness* (2018), a gilding intervention that functions as a "Model For" the future—an operational, generative system designed to process, recontextualize, and transmute the data provided by O’Hanlon.1

The tension between these two works—and the administrative oscillation between shrouding, contextualizing, and attempting to remove the former—demonstrates a fundamental failure in institutional processing. The university administration, in its 2020 decision to remove the O’Hanlon mural following the murder of George Floyd, attempted a "hard delete" of the source code (the O’Hanlon), inadvertently rendering the patch (the Olivier) non-functional.3 This report applies the anthropological framework of "Model Of" (how symbols represent reality) versus "Model For" (how symbols shape reality) to this custodial dilemma.

By viewing the O’Hanlon-Olivier complex not merely as "problematic art" but as a dual-layer dataset, we can construct a "God Prompt"—a master set of custodial instructions that acknowledges the descriptive weight of the past while activating the generative potential of the present. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the visual data, the legal and social controversies, and the custodial mechanisms required to manage historical trauma without engaging in erasure.

### **1.1 The Architecture of Memory: Memorial Hall**

To understand the "Model Of" presented by O’Hanlon, one must first understand the "hardware" in which it is embedded. Memorial Hall, completed in 1929, is a Colonial Revival structure built to honor Kentuckians who died in World War I.5 It is a site of solemnity, a secular temple where the "Model Of" Kentucky citizenship is architecturally codified. The building’s clock tower serves as the visual synecdoche for the university itself, often featured in the institution's logo.5

The space is liminal; it is a vestibule and a lobby, a passageway through which students must transit to attend lectures. It is not a gallery where one chooses to view art; it is an environmental immersion. This architectural context is crucial because the O’Hanlon fresco is not hung *on* the wall; it is *of* the wall. Painted on wet lime plaster, the pigment is chemically bonded to the structural load-bearing walls of the building.5 This material fact transforms the "Model Of" from a file that can be deleted into a structural dependency. To remove the model is to damage the integrity of the archive itself.9

The hall was funded through a statewide subscription campaign beginning in 1919, aiming for $300,000 but raising only $115,000, necessitating a reduced scope that perhaps influenced the modest scale of the lobby where the mural resides.5 Yet, despite its fiscal limitations, the building became the symbolic heart of the campus. It was into this charged space—a memorial to the dead of the Great War—that O’Hanlon introduced her narrative of the state’s living history.

### **1.2 The Theoretical Framework: Geertz in the Lobby**

The "Model Of/Model For" distinction, originally posited by Clifford Geertz, is adapted here for a custodial analysis:

* **Model Of (The Descriptive/Representational):** This is the O’Hanlon mural. It is a representation of reality as perceived by the artist. It renders the structure of Kentucky society in 1934 intelligible to the viewer. It says: "This is how the world is ordered." It captures the stratifications of race, class, and labor as they were understood and normalized in the early 20th century.10 It functions as a diagram of social relations, encoding the "oppressive regimentation" of agrarian labor and the segregation of the era into a static image.1  
* **Model For (The Operational/Generative):** This is the Olivier installation and the desired institutional outcome. It provides a blueprint for psychological and social manipulation. It says: "This is how the world ought to be processed." It takes the raw data of the "Model Of" and provides a mechanism for the viewer to navigate, critique, and transcend that data.2 It is generative because it requires the viewer's active participation to synthesize the gold-leafed figures with the Douglass quote and the original mural.

The current custodial crisis arises because the institution has treated the "Model Of" as a "Model For"—fearing that the mere representation of slavery functions as a generative instruction to perpetuate it, rather than a descriptive archive of its existence.

### **1.3 The Custodial Paradox**

The paradox facing the University of Kentucky is one shared by institutions globally: how to curate historical spaces that encode values now deemed abhorrent. The "hard delete" approach—removal, destruction, or permanent shrouding—attempts to purify the space but ultimately fails by removing the evidence of the very history the institution claims to reject. As Wendell Berry noted in his legal filing and public commentary, removing the mural does not remove the history of slavery; it merely removes the *reminder* of it, thereby enabling a comfortable amnesia.1

Conversely, the "laissez-faire" approach—leaving the work without intervention—fails to protect the current community from the active harm of uncontextualized trauma. The presence of enslaved figures bent in a field, unaccompanied by critique, can be read as a celebration of that subjugation. The "God Prompt" must navigate between these poles, finding a way to *preserve the evidence* (Model Of) while *changing the operation* (Model For).

**2\. The Source Code: Ann Rice O’Hanlon’s "History of Kentucky" (1934)**

Ann Rice O’Hanlon’s fresco is a dense, tiered visual database of Kentucky’s history, stretching forty feet across the Memorial Hall lobby. To construct the "God Prompt," we must first index the data contained within this "Model Of." This requires a granular analysis of the artist, the medium, and the specific visual data points that constitute the controversy.

### **2.1 The Artist and the Commission: A New Deal Archive**

Ann Rice O’Hanlon (1908–1998), a Lexington native and UK graduate, was commissioned under the Treasury Relief Art Project (TRAP), often conflated with the Works Progress Administration (WPA) art projects, though both shared the New Deal’s mission of cultural democratization.13 The commission was part of a broader federal initiative to employ artists and "embellish" public buildings with narratives of American progress and identity.15

O’Hanlon was not a segregationist ideologue in the reactionary sense, but a "liberal" of her time. She later spent much of her life in Marin County, California, teaching at Dominican College where she led integrated classes, and was described by her nephew-in-law Wendell Berry as someone who "never uttered one racist word".1 Her intent, by her own account and the accounts of her family, was to create a comprehensive "Model Of" Kentucky. She utilized a "geologic" approach to history, visualizing the state in layers. The bottom layer depicts the settlement era (pioneers, wilderness); the middle layers show the agrarian and industrial development; the top layers depict contemporary (1930s) society.10

However, the "liberal" intent of 1934 operates within the constraints of 1934 epistemology. O’Hanlon’s "Model Of" Kentucky was one where Black life was visible but strictly confined to roles of labor and service. She did not erase Black people—as many artists of the era did—but she fixed them in a subordinate cosmology. As Berry argues, "Nobody would have objected if she had left them out," implying that their inclusion was an act of visibility, forcing the viewer to acknowledge the labor that built the state.1 Yet, that visibility is filtered through the white gaze, rendering the enslaved figures as anonymous components of the economic machine rather than as individual agents.

### **2.2 The Technical Medium: The Indelible File**

The physical nature of the artwork is a critical variable in the custodial algorithm. O’Hanlon employed the *buon fresco* technique, painting with water-based pigments directly onto wet lime plaster.5 This is not a canvas adhered to a surface; the image is a chemical reaction between the pigment and the wall. As the plaster dries (carbonates), the image becomes an integral part of the architectural substrate.

O’Hanlon’s husband, Dick O’Hanlon, performed the plastering work, laying out fresh plaster each day for Ann to paint.6 This process requires speed and confidence; once the plaster dries, no corrections can be made without chipping out the wall. This technical reality means that "moving" the mural is technically a demolition project. It requires removing the load-bearing wall or facing the high probability of the artwork’s destruction. In the 2020 lawsuit, the court found that removing the mural would likely result in its destruction, a fact that legally barred the university from proceeding with removal under the premise of preservation.8

This physical immutability forces the institution to confront the "Model For" (interpretation) because it cannot easily alter the "Model Of" (the object). The fresco is "read-only" memory; it cannot be rewritten, only annotated.

### **2.3 Visual Data Inventory: Indexing the Layers**

The mural is read from left to right and bottom to top, creating a chronological and developmental narrative. It is a dense visual field, packed with figures representing the social strata of the state.

#### **2.3.1 The Controversial Figures: The "Glitch" in the System**

The "glitch" in the system—the data points that cause the contemporary custodial failure—are located in the specific depictions of marginalized populations. These figures, intended by O’Hanlon as descriptive elements of Kentucky’s history, function today as active transmitters of trauma.

* **The Tobacco Field:** In the central agrarian section, O’Hanlon depicts four enslaved African Americans hunched over, working in a tobacco field.15 Their postures are uniform, heads down, denoting "oppressive regimentation" rather than pastoral leisure.1 They are anonymous mechanics of the harvest, their bodies bent to the service of the crop that defined Kentucky's economy.  
* **The Musicians:** Another section depicts Black musicians playing for White dancers. This imagery has been critiqued as a stereotype of the "happy slave" or the entertainer subservient to white leisure.15 While O’Hanlon may have intended to show Black cultural contribution, the juxtaposition of Black performance for White enjoyment reinforces a hierarchy of service.  
* **The Native American:** A lone Native American figure is depicted with a tomahawk, peering from behind a tree, seemingly threatening a white settler woman.18 This figure reinforces the "savage vs. civilized" narrative of frontier settlement common in 1930s historiography. The figure is lurking, hidden, representing the "danger" of the wilderness that the white pioneers must overcome.20  
* **The Carriage Driver:** An African American figure driving a horse carriage, representing the domestic service role prominent in the antebellum and post-bellum South.12  
* **The Boy in the Tree:** A young Black child is depicted peering down from a tree, often interpreted as a "pickaninny" caricature or simply a marginalized observer of the white social scene unfolding below.12

#### **2.3.2 The Structural Metaphors: Railroad and Progress**

Crucially, the composition places the railroad—a symbol of industrial progress and white mobility—physically *above* the bent backs of the enslaved workers. This vertical arrangement is not accidental. Wendell Berry interprets this not as a celebration of slavery, but as a "bold declaration" that the infrastructure of modern Kentucky rests upon the foundation of Black labor.1

The train cars are filled with white passengers, borne along by the labor occurring in the strata below. This visual structure serves as a Marxist critique (conscious or unconscious) of the extraction economy. The "Model Of" accurately maps the flow of capital: from the bent back of the slave, through the tobacco leaf, to the industrial machinery of the railroad, benefiting the white passenger. To remove the slaves from the picture would be to lie about where the railroad came from.

#### **2.3.3 The Specific Portraits: Wes Littlefield and the Professors**

While the marginalized figures remain anonymous types, the white figures often possess specific identities, reinforcing the disparity in agency within the "Model Of." O’Hanlon included portraits of local Lexington figures.

* **Wes Littlefield:** A local poet known to O’Hanlon, depicted holding a hoe at one end of the fresco.10  
* **The Professors:** O’Hanlon included portraits of University of Kentucky professors who "impressed her with the quality of their faces".10  
* **The Woman with the Book:** At the opposite end from Littlefield, a woman holds a history book and a rake, symbolizing the union of labor and intellect, or perhaps the recording of the history being depicted.10

This uneven distribution of individuality—white figures have names and professions; Black figures have roles and postures—is a critical data point. It documents the erasure of Black individuality in the 1930s consciousness.

### **2.4 The "Sanitization" Debate: Romanticism vs. Realism**

The primary argument for the mural’s removal, articulated by President Eli Capilouto and student protestors, is that the "Model Of" is corrupted by romanticism. They contend that the mural "sanitizes" the brutality of slavery, presenting it as a benign, almost pastoral component of the landscape rather than a moral atrocity.2 The figures are integrated into the rhythm of the composition in a way that aestheticizes their subjugation; the colors are soft, the composition balanced. There are no whips, no chains, no blood.

However, the "Model Of" is arguably accurate in its *banality*. Slavery in Kentucky was not an anomaly but the economic engine of the state. By integrating the enslaved figures into the landscape, O’Hanlon provided a "Model Of" a society where slavery was normative. The horror lies precisely in the *lack* of horror—in the fact that these bent bodies were as much a part of the Kentucky scenery as the limestone and the bluegrass. As Berry argues, the uniform clothing and posture denote "oppressive regimentation," providing visual evidence of the lack of freedom.1

The debate, therefore, is between two readings of the "Model Of":

1. **The Romantic Reading (Capilouto/Critics):** The lack of explicit violence constitutes a lie about the nature of slavery.  
2. **The Structural Reading (Berry/O’Hanlon):** The integration of slavery into the foundation of the state constitutes a truth about the economy of Kentucky.

| Visual Data Point | "Model Of" (1934 Intent/Context) | "Model For" (2024 Reception/Trauma) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Enslaved Field Workers** | Representation of agrarian labor foundation; acknowledgment of Black role in economy. | Symbol of dehumanization; reminder of intergenerational trauma; "sanitized" slavery. |

| **White Portraits (Littlefield)** | Specificity; honoring local intellect and character. | Privilege of individuality; contrast with the anonymity of the Black figures. |

**3\. The Patch: Karyn Olivier’s "Witness" (2018)**

In 2018, the University engaged in a sophisticated custodial strategy. Rather than destroying the "Model Of," they commissioned a "Model For" to run alongside it. Karyn Olivier, a Philadelphia-based Black artist born in Trinidad, was selected by the Memorial Hall Art Committee to create a site-specific installation that would "respond" to the O’Hanlon mural.2

This intervention was an attempt to upgrade the operating system of Memorial Hall without destroying the hard drive. It represents a shift from *subtraction* (hiding the mural) to *addition* (expanding the narrative).

### **3.1 The Algorithm of "Witness": Sampling and Elevation**

Olivier’s work, *Witness*, installed in the vestibule dome immediately preceding the lobby, functions as a data processing filter. Crucially, Olivier did not create new figures from scratch. She engaged in a process of *sampling*. She extracted the specific marginalized figures from O’Hanlon’s fresco—the field workers, the musicians, the Native American, the carriage driver—and reproduced them on the vestibule ceiling.2

This sampling technique is the core of the "Model For." By isolating these figures from the context of white dominance (the "oppressive regimentation" of the field, the threat of the settler), Olivier liberates them. She places them against a background of gold leaf, a material associated with Byzantine religious icons, Renaissance altarpieces, and the divine.2

### **3.2 Gold Leaf as Value Inversion**

The gold leaf performs a "value inversion" operation. In O’Hanlon’s "Model Of," the figures are lowly, bent, and peripheral. In Olivier’s "Model For," they are elevated, central, and divine.

* **Elevation:** The viewer must physically look *up* to see them. This shifts the viewer's physical posture from one of looking *at* (or down on) the figures to looking *up to* them.2  
* **Rebirth:** Olivier describes the gold as elevating the oppressed from "the lowly to the divine." It creates a sacred space where the anonymous figures are granted a spiritual agency they are denied in the secular history of the lobby.12  
* **Contextual Agency:** By removing the white figures, the Black and Indigenous figures are no longer defined by their labor for others but by their existence as human beings. The gold leaf acts as a "halo," sanctifying their suffering and their endurance.

### **3.3 The Narrative Expansion: Naming the Anonymous**

Olivier also added new data to the system to counter the anonymity of O’Hanlon’s Black figures. Around the base of the dome, she inscribed a quote by Frederick Douglass: *"There is not a man beneath the canopy of heaven, that does not know that slavery is wrong for him"*.2 This text provides the moral clarity that critics found lacking in O’Hanlon’s work.

Furthermore, she included four medallions depicting specific, named historical figures who were absent from O’Hanlon’s generalized typology but present in Kentucky history. These portraits transition the "Model Of" from *anonymous labor* to *specific agency*:

1. **Chief Red Bird:** A Cherokee leader who lived peacefully among settlers in Clay County before being murdered by two Tennessee men. His inclusion counters the "violent savage" narrative of the O’Hanlon tomahawk figure with a narrative of peaceful coexistence and white betrayal.2  
2. **Charlotte Dupuy:** An enslaved woman who filed a freedom suit in 1829 against her enslaver, Henry Clay (then Secretary of State). Her inclusion highlights legal resistance and agency within the system of slavery.12  
3. **Peter Durrett:** A former slave and preacher who founded the First African Baptist Church in Lexington. He represents spiritual leadership and community building.2  
4. **Georgia Davis Powers:** The first African American Kentucky state senator, elected in 1967\. She represents the trajectory from enslavement to political power, completing the timeline O’Hanlon stopped in 1934\.24

### **3.4 The Interdependency of the Models**

Crucially, Olivier argues that her work is *dependent* on the O’Hanlon mural. "Witness" is a call-and-response; without the call, the response is meaningless. If the O’Hanlon mural is removed, the "displaced" figures in the dome lose their semantic anchor. They become free-floating decoration rather than a critique of the specific images in the next room.3

Olivier explicitly stated, "My work is dependent on that history... I think in one fell swoop, you've censored me as well".26 She argued that removing the O’Hanlon mural renders her work "blind and mute," stripping it of its power to confront and transform the past. This highlights the fragility of the "Model For"—it requires the persistence of the "Model Of" to function as a corrective tool.

**4\. System Failure: The Administrative Purge and the Legal Firewall**

The custodial crisis reached its peak in 2020\. Following the murder of George Floyd and the global reckoning with symbols of white supremacy, University President Eli Capilouto announced the removal of the O’Hanlon mural, citing the need to remove a "barrier" to inclusivity.8 This decision represents a failure to distinguish between the "Model Of" (the artifact) and the "Model For" (the institutional value system).

### **4.1 The Administrative Logic (The "Hard Delete")**

The administration’s logic was binary and reactive. Capilouto argued that the mural "sanitizes" history and serves as a painful daily reminder to Black students that their ancestors were enslaved.1 The university prioritized the *immediate emotional safety* of the student body over the *archival integrity* of the space or the *dialogic potential* of the Olivier artwork.

The university spokesman, Jay Blanton, stated, "The mural has been a hurdle, one we've not been able to clear in terms of being able to move beyond it".8 This language suggests a desire to "move beyond" history by erasing its representation, a "Model For" grounded in *cleanliness* and *comfort* rather than *confrontation* and *complexity*.

### **4.2 The Legal Firewall (The Berry Lawsuit)**

The "hard delete" was blocked by a lawsuit filed by Wendell Berry, the renowned Kentucky author and agrarian philosopher, and his wife Tanya Amyx Berry, who is O’Hanlon’s niece and oldest living heir.8 The lawsuit, filed in Franklin Circuit Court, operated on the "Public Trust" doctrine and the specific legal status of New Deal art.

* **State Property & Federal Commission:** The mural was funded by the federal government (PWAP) and is held in trust by the University for the people of Kentucky. Berry argued it is not the private property of the administration to destroy.24 The lawsuit contended that the federal government gave the state limited rights to the artwork, which might transfer back if the state chose not to display it.29  
* **Anti-Censorship:** Berry framed the removal as censorship and a destruction of history. He argued that removing the mural destroys the evidence of the crime (slavery) rather than confronting it. "Black people *did* work in tobacco fields," Berry wrote. To remove the image is to deny the historical fact.1 He rejected the comparison to statues of Confederate generals, viewing the mural instead as a depiction of *what happened* rather than a celebration of *who ruled*.  
* **The Ruling:** In March 2024, Judge Thomas D. Wingate dismissed the lawsuit on the grounds of standing (the Berrys did not suffer a concrete "injury in fact" different from the general public). However, the ruling contained a critical caveat that effectively *protected* the artwork. The judge found that removing the mural would likely result in its destruction due to its fresco nature (being part of the wall). Therefore, the university was legally bound to *maintain* the mural, as destroying state property of such significance would be unlawful.9  
* **Protecting Olivier:** The judge also explicitly noted the relationship between the two works, writing that Olivier’s "Witness" offers an outstanding response and that her purpose would be "fractured" by removing the O’Hanlon mural.9

### **4.3 The Custodial Paradox and the Closed Door**

The university is now locked in a paradox. It cannot destroy the mural (legal/structural constraint), but it refuses to display it (social/political constraint). Memorial Hall has been closed to the public since 2020, and the doors locked, effectively "shrouding" the building itself.8

This is a "suspension" of the system—a refusal to run the "Model Of" or the "Model For." By locking the doors, the university has inadvertently censored both O’Hanlon *and* Olivier. The "Witness" cannot witness anything if no one is permitted to enter. The "God Prompt" must provide a way to unlock the doors.

**5\. The Dual-Layer "God Prompt"**

To resolve this paradox, we must construct a custodial framework—a "God Prompt"—that allows the institution to operate both models simultaneously without crashing the system. This prompt distinguishes between the *Descriptive Layer* (what is there) and the *Generative Layer* (what we do with it). It functions as the master algorithm for the university's cultural heritage management.

### **5.1 The Conceptual Prompt Structure**

**System Role:** Institutional Custodian & Critical Pedagogue

**Primary Objective:** Manage the O'Hanlon-Olivier Complex to maximize historical literacy, critical thinking, and social agency, while minimizing uncontextualized trauma.

**Hard Constraint:** Erasure or destruction of the "Model Of" (O'Hanlon) is prohibited. Silencing of the "Model For" (Olivier) is prohibited.

**Layer 1: The Descriptive Model (The "Model Of")**

* **Input Data:** O’Hanlon Fresco (1934).  
* **Function:** Archive. Witness. Evidence.  
* **Operational Instruction:** Acknowledge the mural as a primary source document of 1934\. It is not a window into *actual* 1800s Kentucky, but a window into *1930s perceptions* of 1800s Kentucky. It documents how the New Deal era visualized race, labor, and progress.  
* **Metadata Tagging:** \#JimCrow \#WPA \#AgrarianLabor \#SegregatedUniversity \#WhiteGaze \#StructuralInequality.  
* **Warning Protocol:** Do not optimize for comfort. Optimize for accuracy. The pain caused by the image is data regarding the toxicity of the history it depicts. The "shroud" is a denial of service; the "view" is an encounter with evidence.

**Layer 2: The Generative Model (The "Model For")**

* **Input Data:** Olivier Installation (2018) \+ Student Protest Data (2015-2020) \+ Legal Ruling (2024).  
* **Function:** Synthesis. Critique. Agency.  
* **Operational Instruction:** Use the O’Hanlon data as the raw material for inquiry. The institution must not "endorse" the O’Hanlon worldview but *host* it as the subject of the Olivier critique. The O'Hanlon is the *text*; the Olivier is the *commentary*.  
* **Mechanism:** The physical passage from the Vestibule (Olivier) to the Lobby (O’Hanlon) is a passage from *Idealism/Agency* to *Realism/Labor*. The viewer is primed by the "Model For" (divinity of the Black figure) before confronting the "Model Of" (subjugation of the Black figure). This sequence is non-negotiable.

**Layer 3: The Custodial Output (The Synthesis)**

* **Action:** Re-open Memorial Hall.  
* **Protocol:** "Active Curation." The space is no longer a passive lobby; it is an active classroom. Access is mediated by the "Model For."  
* **Narrative:** "We do not hide the past; we confront it with the present to build the future."

The following outputs translate the "God Prompt" into tangible administrative tools, designed to operationalize the theory in the physical space of the university.

### **6.1 Museum Labels (The Interface)**

Standard museum labels are insufficient here. We need a dual-label system that addresses the "Model Of" and "Model For," placed strategically to guide the viewer's cognitive processing.

#### **Label A: Mounted in the Vestibule (The Olivier Lens)**

**Location:** Central Vestibule, beneath the Gold Leaf Dome.

**Title:** *Witness: The Golden Dome*

**Artist:** Karyn Olivier (b. 1968), installed 2018\.

**The Model For:**

"You are standing beneath a reconstruction of memory. The figures floating in gold leaf above you—the musicians, the field workers, the mother and child—were not invented by Karyn Olivier. They were rescued. In the next room, you will find these same figures locked into the rigid hierarchy of Ann Rice O’Hanlon’s 1934 fresco. There, they are defined by their labor and their service to a segregated society. Here, Olivier has extracted them, stripped away the landscape of oppression, and placed them in the realm of the divine.

**Operational Instruction:** Look up. Witness their humanity. Carry this image of divinity with you as you enter the lobby. You are now equipped to view the past not as it was dictated to them, but as it belongs to us.

**Location:** Lobby Wall, adjacent to the Fresco.

**Title:** *History of Kentucky: An Archive of 1934*

**Artist:** Ann Rice O’Hanlon (1908–1998), painted 1934\.

**The Model Of:**

"This fresco does not merely depict the history of Kentucky; it depicts the worldview of 1934\. Painted during the era of segregation, when Black students were barred from entering this university, O’Hanlon’s work captures the social order of her time. This wall is not a monument to be celebrated, but a document to be read. It is evidence of the world we have inherited, and the world Karyn Olivier’s *Witness* asks us to dismantle.

**Custodial Note:** This artwork is preserved as part of the historical record. Its presence is not an endorsement of its hierarchy, but a commitment to the truth of our institutional history."

### **6.2 Policy Memo (The Administrative Code)**

**To:** Board of Trustees, University of Kentucky

**From:** Office of Cultural Heritage Strategy & Legal Counsel

**Subject:** *Operational Directive: Re-Opening Memorial Hall via "Active Curation"*

**Executive Summary:**

The current policy of locking Memorial Hall constitutes a failure of our educational mission and a *de facto* censorship of Karyn Olivier’s *Witness*. Pursuant to the 2024 Franklin Circuit Court ruling, the O’Hanlon mural cannot be removed. Therefore, we must transition from a strategy of *avoidance* to a strategy of *engagement*. We propose the "Active Curation" policy.

**Policy Directives:**

1. **Controlled Access Flow:** The architectural flow must be strictly managed. Visitors and students must enter through the Vestibule (Olivier) to establish the critical framework ("Model For") before engaging the Lobby (O’Hanlon) ("Model Of"). The side entrances should be restricted or clearly signed to redirect traffic through the Vestibule.  
2. **The "Living Syllabus" Protocol:** The mural is no longer "decor." It is designated as a "primary source." Classes in history, sociology, African American studies, and art history are granted priority access. The space must be treated as a laboratory for the study of historical memory, not a passive hallway.  
3. **Lighting as Editorial Tool:** Lighting design will be updated. During non-instructional times, lighting should highlight the Olivier dome (using warm, upward-cast spots) while keeping the O’Hanlon mural in ambient, dimmer light. This visual hierarchy signals institutional prioritization of the *generative* model over the *descriptive* model without altering the physical structure.  
4. **Digital Overlay (AR Initiative):** Develop an Augmented Reality (AR) application accessible via smartphone. When a student points their device at the "Native American with Tomahawk" in the mural, the app overlays the portrait of *Chief Red Bird* from the Olivier installation, along with biographical data. This "virtual patch" allows for the correction of the narrative without the destruction of the artifact.  
5. **Advisory Council:** Establish a permanent student-faculty council to review the contextual materials annually, ensuring the "Model For" evolves with the student body’s needs.

### **6.3 AI Prompts (Generative Educational Tools)**

To demonstrate the difference in custodial responsibility to an AI (which might act as a digital docent or educational bot), we must differentiate the prompts based on the model being accessed.

**Prompt A: The "Model Of" (Descriptive Analysis)**

**Role:** Historical Analyst

**Task:** Analyze the visual data of Ann Rice O’Hanlon’s 1934 "History of Kentucky" fresco.

**Instructions:** Catalog the spatial relationships between figures. Specifically, identify the vertical positioning of the railroad cars relative to the field workers. Correlate this visual hierarchy with 1930s labor statistics in Kentucky and the PWAP's artistic guidelines regarding "American Scene" painting.

**Output:** A report on how "progress" is visually dependent on "subjugation" in this composition. Do not interpret the figures' emotions; interpret their structural function in the economy of the image.

**Prompt B: The "Model For" (Generative Synthesis)**

**Role:** Critical Pedagogue & Ethics Officer

**Task:** Using Karyn Olivier’s "Witness" as the ethical framework, generate a lesson plan for 1st-year undergraduates.

**Objective:** Transform the "trauma" of the O’Hanlon tobacco field imagery into "agency" using the Olivier intervention.

**Constraint:** Do not hide the O’Hanlon image. The critique requires the object.

**Instructions:** Create a dialogue script where the "divine" figures in Olivier’s dome speak to their 1934 counterparts in the fresco. What does the liberated figure tell the subjugated figure about their future? Use the Frederick Douglass quote ("...slavery is wrong for him") as the thematic bridge.

**Output:** A discussion guide that leads students from the *observation* of the fresco to the *action* of the installation.

**7\. Conclusion: The Responsible Custodian**

The conflict at the University of Kentucky is not merely about a "racist mural." It is a conflict about the function of the university itself. If the university is a "Model Of" safety, it deletes the mural to prevent pain. But if the university is a "Model For" critical thinking, it retains the mural as a necessary, if painful, object of study.

The legal ruling of 2024 has settled the question of the mural's physical existence: it stays.9 The question now is its social existence. Wendell Berry’s lawsuit and Karyn Olivier’s artwork, though coming from vastly different cultural standpoints (the white agrarian traditionalist and the black urban conceptualist), converge on a single truth: **You cannot fix the future by hiding the past.**

Olivier’s work proves that the "Model Of" (O’Hanlon) is required fuel for the "Model For" (Witness). By destroying the O’Hanlon, the University would not be erasing racism; it would be erasing the context required to understand Olivier’s triumph. The gold leaf needs the lime plaster to shine against. The custodial responsibility, therefore, is to maintain the tension—to keep the wound visible so that the healing (the gold leaf, the dialogue, the policy) has a place to work.

The "God Prompt" proposed here does not delete the error; it highlights it, annotates it, and builds a better system on top of it. It requires the University of Kentucky to be brave enough to unlock the doors of Memorial Hall and trust its students to navigate the space between the wall and the dome, between the history that was imposed upon them and the history they will write for themselves.
