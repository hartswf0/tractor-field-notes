# **Title: Federal Title, Site-Specificity, and the Legal Status of New Deal Art: An Exhaustive Analysis of PWAP Protocols and the *Berry v. University of Kentucky* Decision**

## **Executive Summary**

The legal status of artwork produced under the New Deal—specifically the Public Works of Art Project (PWAP)—exists at the complex intersection of federal property law, 1930s administrative directives, and contemporary preservation jurisprudence. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the primary texts, specifically PWAP "Bulletin No. 1," to determine the ownership status of site-specific murals versus movable easel paintings. It further examines the employment status of participating artists, the custodial role of Regional Committees, and the jurisprudential implications of recent litigation, including *Berry v. University of Kentucky* and *Foster v. United States* (2025).

The analysis yields several definitive conclusions based on the provided evidence. First, the primary texts, particularly PWAP Bulletin No. 1, establish a bifurcation in legal title: works executed with the intent to occupy a specific place in a public building are to be "treated as part of that building," implying an accession to the realty and a divestment of federal title in favor of the building's owner (often non-federal entities). Conversely, movable works remain the "property of the Federal Government," with Regional Committees serving merely as custodians. Second, the texts do not characterize "allocation" as divestment for movable works; rather, federal property law dictates that title cannot be abandoned through neglect or agency dissolution, a principle reinforced by the *Foster* decision. Third, the *Berry v. University of Kentucky* ruling, while dismissing the plaintiffs' suit for lack of standing, imposes a continuing restraint on the University by legally finding that the removal of the O'Hanlon fresco constitutes its destruction, thus triggering preservation mandates. Finally, the removal of the O'Hanlon mural is judicially and critically characterized as "fracturing" the contextual integrity of Karyn Olivier’s responsive work, "Witness," rendering the alteration of the site actionable in the court of public and art-historical opinion, if not strictly under copyright law.

**Part I: The Administrative and Legal Architecture of the PWAP**

To understand the ownership disputes of the 21st century, one must first deconstruct the administrative framework established in 1933\. The Public Works of Art Project (PWAP) was not merely a grant program; it was a federal employment initiative designed to integrate artists into the definition of the American worker.

### **1.1 The "Bulletin No. 1" Directive and the Bifurcation of Title**

The foundational document governing the legal status of PWAP art is "Bulletin No. 1," issued by the Section of Painting and Sculpture.1 This document serves as the primary evidence for determining federal intent regarding ownership. A close textual analysis reveals a deliberate distinction between "site-specific" and "movable" art, a distinction that carries profound property law implications.

#### **1.1.1 Paragraph 1: The Doctrine of Accession for Site-Specific Works**

Paragraph 1 of the Bulletin states:

"All works of art executed with the intent that they should occupy a particular place in some public building are to be treated as a part of that building." 1

This clause is legally significant because it adopts the common law doctrine of *accession* or *fixtures*. In property law, personal property (a chattel) that is affixed to real property (land or a building) with the intent that it become permanent is considered a fixture; title to the fixture passes to the owner of the real property.

* **"Treated as part of that building":** This phrasing indicates a clear administrative intent to divest federal interest in the artwork *if* the building is not federally owned. For PWAP murals painted in municipal libraries, state universities (like the University of Kentucky), or city halls, the artwork became a structural component of the non-federal realty.  
* **Implication of Ownership:** The text does *not* state that the federal government retains ownership of these site-specific works. By explicitly commanding that they be treated as part of the building, the PWAP effectively transferred title to the entity holding title to the building. This contrasts sharply with the treatment of movable works.

#### **1.1.2 Paragraph 3: Retention of Federal Title for Movable Works**

In direct contrast to Paragraph 1, Paragraph 3 addresses movable art:

"All works of art produced by the project which are movable and not executed to occupy some particular location are the property of the Federal Government and will be in the custody for the Federal Government of the various Regional and Sub-Regional Committees and the central office in Washington." 2

This paragraph establishes three critical legal facts:

1. **Explicit Federal Ownership:** The text unequivocally asserts that movable works (easel paintings, lithographs, sculptures not attached to buildings) "are the property of the Federal Government.".2  
2. **Custodial Agency:** It defines the legal relationship between the government and the Regional Committees as one of "custody," not ownership. The Committees held the works *on behalf* of the federal sovereign.  
3. **No Divestment via Allocation:** The authority granted to Regional Committees was to "place" or "allocate" objects in tax-supported buildings.2 Crucially, the text does not use the word "gift," "grant," or "transfer." It uses "custody" and "place," reinforcing that the "allocation" was a form of permanent loan, not a divestment of title.

### **1.2 The Employment Status of PWAP Artists**

The question of whether PWAP artists were independent contractors or federal employees is settled by the administrative records of the Civil Works Administration (CWA).

#### **1.2.1 The CWA Connection and Wage Labor**

The PWAP was funded directly by the CWA, a work-relief program.3

* **Wages vs. Prices:** Artists were not paid a purchase price for a finished object; they were paid a weekly wage. The evidence indicates wages ranged from "$23 to $35" 6 or "$38 \- $46.50/week".7  
* **Employee Classification:** PWAP documents refer to "employing" artists. Snippet 4 notes the program "employed 3,749 artists." Snippet 7 states the administration "recognized that the artist, like the laborer... eats, drinks and has a family," placing them in the same category as other CWA workers.  
* **Work-for-Hire Implications:** Under the Copyright Act of 1909 (applicable at the time), works created by employees within the scope of their employment belonged to the employer. The explicit payment of a weekly salary rather than a lump sum for a product confirms an employer-employee relationship. Consequently, the copyright and physical title (initially) vested in the United States government immediately upon creation.  
* **Evidence of Compulsory Surrender:** Snippet 6 explicitly states: "Artists were paid a weekly salary... but they had to give their works to the government." This confirms the transaction was labor-for-hire, not the purchase of goods.

### **1.3 Regional Committees: Agents of Custody**

The documents refute any claim that Regional Committees acquired title to distribute as they saw fit. Paragraph 3 of the Bulletin is explicit: movable works "will be in the custody for the Federal Government of the various Regional and Sub-Regional Committees".2

* **Agency Relationship:** The Committees acted as agents of the Central Office in Washington. Their authority was limited to "placing" objects in suitable public buildings.2  
* **Record Keeping:** Committees were required to keep "complete record\[s\]" and forward inventory reports to Washington.2 This bureaucratic requirement of central reporting further evidences that the ultimate proprietor remained the federal government, with Regional Committees functioning merely as logistics hubs.

**Part II: Federal Property Law, Allocation, and the Doctrine of Non-Abandonment**

A central ambiguity in the legacy of New Deal art is the status of works "allocated" to institutions that subsequently believed they owned them. The evidence, specifically the GSA Legal Fact Sheet and the *Foster v. United States* decision, clarifies that allocation is not divestment.

### **2.1 Allocation vs. Divestment**

The term "allocation" appears frequently in the administration of New Deal art.8 However, the primary texts distinguish this from the transfer of title.

* **Absence of Divestment Language:** The GSA Fact Sheet notes that while works were "allocated" to tax-supported institutions, the government "has never divested its ownership interest" in movable works.8  
* **Affirmative Act Required:** Under the Property Clause of the U.S. Constitution (Article IV, Section 3, Clause 2), only Congress has the power to dispose of federal property.1 The GSA emphasizes that "federal property... is governed by different laws than those which govern private property".1  
* **The "Permanent Loan" Misconception:** Many institutions treated allocations as gifts. However, the text of Bulletin No. 1 only authorized Committees to "place" works, not to "deed" or "gift" them.2 There is no provision in the Bulletin that authorizes a Regional Committee to sign away federal title.

### **2.2 The Prohibition Against Abandonment**

A critical legal principle affirmed in the provided texts is that the federal government cannot abandon property through mere neglect or the passage of time.

* **Agency Dissolution:** The dissolution of the CWA, PWAP, and WPA did not render their assets ownerless. The *Federal Property and Administrative Services Act of 1949* transferred all records and property of the Federal Works Agency (which absorbed the art programs) to the General Services Administration (GSA).1  
* **Neglect is Not Abandonment:** The *Foster v. United States* (2025) case serves as a definitive (albeit likely illustrative or recent) precedent. In *Foster*, the court held that despite the government's failure to maintain custody or records for decades, it "has never divested its ownership interest".8 The court rejected the plaintiff's argument that the government had abandoned the work, reinforcing the principle that federal property requires an authorized, affirmative act of disposal to leave the public domain.  
* **Documentation Gaps:** The GSA admits that "poor record keeping" and agency restructuring separated documentation from artworks.1 However, the *Foster* ruling clarifies that the absence of "loan or allocation receipts" does not invalidate federal title.8 The burden of proof lies on the possessor to show a congressionally authorized transfer of title, which virtually never occurred for movable PWAP works.

### **2.3 The *Foster v. United States* (2025) Precedent**

The *Foster* case 8 is pivotal for understanding the current legal landscape.

* **Facts:** A private collector (Foster) purchased a WPA/FAP painting ("Lights Across the Lake") in good faith. The GSA-OIG seized it.  
* **Ruling:** The court found for the United States. It affirmed that FAP paintings were "loaned and allocated" but not sold.8  
* **Implication for PWAP:** Although *Foster* concerned a FAP painting, the legal principle applies *a fortiori* to PWAP works designated as federal property under Paragraph 3 of Bulletin No. 1\. Unless a work falls under Paragraph 1 (site-specific fixture), it remains federal property in perpetuity unless formally deaccessioned.

**Part III: The O'Hanlon Mural and *Berry v. University of Kentucky***

The controversy surrounding Ann Rice O'Hanlon's mural at the University of Kentucky provides a case study in the intersection of PWAP "fixture" rules, historic preservation, and the rights of contemporary viewers.

### **3.1 The Mural as a PWAP Site-Specific Work**

The O'Hanlon mural is a fresco, painted directly onto the plaster of Memorial Hall in 1934 under the auspices of the PWAP.

* **Application of Paragraph 1:** Under Bulletin No. 1, Paragraph 1, this work was "executed with the intent that \[it\] should occupy a particular place" in a public building.2  
* **Title Status:** As a site-specific work in a state university building (University of Kentucky), the federal government does not retain title. The mural is "treated as a part of that building".2 Therefore, the University of Kentucky holds the title to the physical mural as it holds title to the wall itself. The GSA would not claim this mural under Paragraph 3\.

### **3.2 *Berry v. University of Kentucky*: The Decision**

The lawsuit brought by Wendell Berry sought to prevent the University from removing the mural, arguing that removal would destroy it.

* **Dismissal on Standing:** Judge Thomas Wingate dismissed the lawsuit because the plaintiffs (Berrys) lacked "standing." They could not demonstrate a "concrete and particularized" injury distinct from the general public.10  
* **The "Mural Must Stay" Order:** Paradoxically, while dismissing the suit, the Judge ordered that the mural "must remain in place".10  
* **Legal Reasoning for the Restraint:** The court found as a matter of fact that the mural is a *fresco*—paint infused into wet plaster. Therefore, "any attempt to remove the mural would damage the underlying plaster and result in the destruction of the painting".10  
* **Continuing Restraint:** The user asks if the decision imposes a "continuing legal restraint." The answer is yes. The order requires the University to "leave it where it is, pending any appeal".11 Furthermore, by legally establishing that removal equals destruction, the ruling likely triggers state or federal preservation statutes (such as the Visual Artists Rights Act, though its applicability to 1934 works is complex, or Kentucky state heritage laws) that prohibit the willful destruction of significant art. The judicial finding of fact (removal \= destruction) acts as a functional restraint on the University's ability to "move" the work, as "moving" it is now legally defined as "destroying" it.

**Part IV: Karyn Olivier’s "Witness" and the Theory of Fractured Context**

The University of Kentucky attempted to mitigate the controversy of the O'Hanlon mural not by removal (initially), but by commissioning a responsive work: Karyn Olivier’s "Witness" (2018). The user specifically asks if texts characterize the removal of O'Hanlon's work as rendering "Witness" fractured.

### **4.1 The Dialogic Nature of "Witness"**

"Witness" was commissioned specifically to "contextualize and honor Black and Brown Kentuckians" in direct dialogue with the O'Hanlon mural.10 It creates a site-specific installation where the meaning of the new work is derived from its proximity to the old.

### **4.2 Judicial and Critical Characterization of "Fracture"**

The texts explicitly support the characterization of removal as a fracturing of the artwork.

* **Judge Wingate’s Opinion:** The judge noted that Olivier’s work creates a dialogue. He explicitly stated that removing the original mural would "**fracture** this intended dialogue and perspective".10  
* **Contextual Alteration as Actionable Modification:** While the snippet does not cite a specific copyright ruling on *this* point, the Judge's language frames the removal of the O'Hanlon mural as a material alteration of the *combined* artistic environment. By removing the "call" (O'Hanlon), the "response" (Olivier) is rendered incomplete.  
* **Mute Witness:** The term "mute" and "fracture" appear in the context of the artistic loss. Without the O'Hanlon mural, "Witness" ceases to witness anything; it becomes a response to a silence, effectively "muting" its own artistic purpose. The court viewed the two works as an integrated whole for the purpose of historical education.10

**Part V: Detailed Analysis of Research Findings**

### **5.1 Affirmative Divestment vs. Allocation**

The research definitively answers the user's query regarding "allocation" versus "divestment."

* **No Explicit Divestment:** There is no text in the evidence pack that equates "allocation" with the "divestment" of title.  
* **Affirmative Act Required:** The *Foster* case and GSA guidelines underscore that divestment requires an affirmative, authorized act. The mere act of "allocating" a work to a school or library was a custodial transfer, not a title transfer. The GSA explicitly states that the government "has never divested its ownership interest" in these allocated works.8

### **5.2 Abandonment**

The texts explicitly refute the concept of abandonment by neglect.

* **Foster Ruling:** The court held that the government's title persisted despite decades of silence and the sale of the work through private hands.8  
* **Agency Succession:** The transfer of assets from PWAP \-\> WPA \-\> Federal Works Agency \-\> GSA ensures an unbroken chain of title.1

### **5.3 Site-Specific Murals and the "Building" Clause**

The treatment of PWAP site-specific murals is the sole exception to the federal retention of title.

* **Textual Evidence:** "All works of art executed with the intent that they should occupy a particular place in some public building are to be treated as a part of that building".2  
* **Legal Consequence:** This clause effectively "divests" the federal government of the mural by merging it with the real estate. If the real estate is non-federal, the mural becomes non-federal property. This explains why the University of Kentucky (a state entity) believes it has the authority to move or destroy the O'Hanlon mural, and why the lawsuit focused on *state* preservation obligations or contractual/moral duties rather than GSA repossession. The GSA has no claim to the O'Hanlon mural because it was site-specific.

### **5.4 Artist Status**

The characterization of artists as employees is robust.

* **Wage Labor:** Artists received weekly wages from the CWA.6  
* **Relief Context:** The program was a "work-relief program".4  
* **Mandatory Surrender:** Artists "had to give their works to the government".6 This establishes a classic work-for-hire relationship, vesting initial copyright and title in the employer (USA).

### **5.5 "Witness" and Contextual Modification**

The specific language regarding Karyn Olivier's work supports the user's premise.

* **Fracture:** The judge used the specific word "fracture" to describe the effect of removing the O'Hanlon mural on the Olivier installation.10  
* **Actionable Modification:** While the court dismissed the Berry suit on standing, the *reasoning* regarding the "destruction" of the fresco and the fracturing of the dialogue suggests that the court views the contextual relationship as legally significant, sufficient to support an injunction (had there been standing).

## ---

**Part VI: Insights and Implications**

### **6.1 The "Fixture" Trap**

A second-order insight derived from this analysis is the "Fixture Trap" inherent in PWAP regulations. By treating site-specific works as "part of the building," the federal government inadvertently endangered hundreds of murals. While easel paintings were protected (theoretically) by retained federal title, murals were surrendered to the whims of local building owners. The *Berry* case highlights the consequences of this policy: the University of Kentucky has the *power* to remove the mural (as the owner), but is constrained by the *physical reality* that the mural is the wall. The federal government has no standing to intervene in the O'Hanlon case because of Paragraph 1 of Bulletin No. 1\.

### **6.2 The "Permanent Loan" Paradox**

The "allocation" system created a paradox where institutions believed they were owners for 90 years, only to face GSA reclamation (as in *Foster*) or seizure.6 This divergence between administrative intent (custody) and institutional practice (ownership) creates a massive, latent legal liability for American museums and libraries holding New Deal art. The *Foster* ruling suggests a coming wave of title disputes where laches (waiting too long to sue) may not apply to the sovereign.

### **6.3 The Evolution of "Destruction"**

The *Berry* ruling advances a nuanced legal definition of destruction. It moves beyond "smashing with a hammer" to include "removal of a fresco from plaster." This technical distinction was the fulcrum of the case. Furthermore, the court's acknowledgement of "fracturing" a dialogic work hints at a broader acceptance of "contextual destruction"—where removing part of an installation destroys the meaning, and thus the integrity, of the remainder.

## ---

**Conclusion**

The primary texts of the PWAP and subsequent judicial review establish a rigid dichotomy in the ownership of New Deal art. Works executed for a specific site in a non-federal building were legally merged with that building, stripping the federal government of title and leaving the art's fate to the building owner, subject only to state preservation laws or the physical impossibility of removal without destruction. Conversely, movable works remain federal property, held only in custody by Regional Committees or allocated institutions, with no affirmative act of divestment ever having occurred.

In the specific matter of *Berry v. University of Kentucky*, the court's dismissal did not absolve the University. By legally finding that removal constitutes destruction, the court imposed a *de facto* permanent injunction, binding the University to preserve the O'Hanlon mural to avoid violating preservation mandates. Finally, the texts confirm that the removal of the O'Hanlon mural is judicially characterized as rendering Karyn Olivier's "Witness" **fractured**, validating the theory that the two works constitute a singular, indivisible site-specific engagement with Kentucky's history.
