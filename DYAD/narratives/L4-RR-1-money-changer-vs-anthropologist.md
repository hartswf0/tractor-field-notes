# L4-RR-1: THE MONEY-CHANGER vs THE ANTHROPOLOGIST

'I have a watch that belonged to a peasant,' THE MONEY-CHANGER says. 'What is it worth?'

THE ANTHROPOLOGIST takes the watch. He turns it over in his hands. He does not look at the mechanism. He looks at the scratches, the worn leather band, the faded inscription in a language he cannot read.

'Wrong question,' The Anthropologist says. 'You ask what it is *worth*. I ask what it *means*.'

The Money-Changer leans forward. He has defeated the Troublemaker by proving that noise without exchange has no consequence. Now he faces someone who does not traffic in exchange at all.

'Meaning does not feed people,' The Money-Changer says. 'The peasant sold the watch for bread. The bread kept him alive. That is what exchange does. It translates objects into survival.'

'The bread is gone,' The Anthropologist replies. 'The peasant is dead. But the watch is still here. And I can still read his life in the scratches. I can still feel the weight of his days in the worn leather. You traded bread; I inherit civilization.'

The Money-Changer is silent. He is not used to opponents who refuse his terms.

'You are a translator,' The Anthropologist continues. 'You move objects from hand to hand, extracting value at each step. Very elegant. But translation is not understanding. You know the price; I know the *story*. And when the price is forgotten, the story remains.'

'Stories do not circulate,' The Money-Changer objects. 'They are stuck in place. They are local. I am global. I can take this watch from here to Constantinople and still sell it.'

'You can sell the object,' The Anthropologist agrees. 'But you cannot sell the meaning. In Constantinople, it is just metal. Here, it is a life. That is the difference between us: you make things fungible; I make them thick.'

The Money-Changer picks up another object—a coin, ancient and worn. 'And what is the story of this?'

The Anthropologist examines it. 'Give me a year. Give me access to the archive. Give me the tomb where this was found. Then I will tell you more than your market ever could.'

'A year is too long,' The Money-Changer says. 'I have trades to make today.'

'Then you will make your trades,' The Anthropologist replies. 'And I will write the book that your grandchildren will read to understand what you did.'

The Money-Changer sets down the coin. For the first time, he feels the weight of time.

'We are not enemies,' The Anthropologist says. 'But we are not the same. You move value. I create meaning. Both are necessary. But only one survives the death of the market.'
