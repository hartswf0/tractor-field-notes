# R3-03: THE PILOT vs THE FUNCTIONARY

'You cannot invent a new type of turn,' THE FUNCTIONARY says.

THE PILOT's hands tighten on the yoke. He is banking through a gap in the clouds, feeling the wind shear through his fingertips. He has defeated the Theorist by proving that local knowledge—the feel of the stall, the read of the sky—cannot be written in a manual. But the Functionary is sitting beside him, and he is not looking at the sky due. He is looking at the yoke.

'I can turn however I want,' The Pilot says. 'I can invert. I can spiral. I can—'

'You can do what the aircraft allows,' The Functionary interrupts. 'Left, right, up, down, fast, slow. You are a master player. But you are playing a game you did not design. The aircraft is the apparatus. You are the function.'

The Pilot pulls back on the yoke, climbing. The engine whines. The clouds fall away. 'I feel the stall before the gauges show it,' he says. 'I read the wind with my body. That is not a function. That is craft.'

'Craft is a function of the apparatus,' The Functionary replies. He pulls out a camera and takes a picture of the sky. *Click.* 'The apparatus defines the range of possible moves. You can exhaust that range—you can become a virtuoso—but you cannot exceed it. Can you make the plane sing opera?'

The Pilot is silent.

'Can you make the plane disappear and reappear in Paris? Can you make the plane love you?'

'Those are absurd,' The Pilot says.

'Exactly. They are outside the ontology of the machine. The apparatus is a box of possible games. You are playing all of them beautifully. But you are still inside the box.'

The Pilot looks at the yoke, the pedals, the gauges. He has always felt free up here—free from the ground, free from the bureaucrat, free from the plan. But the Functionary is showing him the invisible walls.

'Then what is freedom?' The Pilot asks.

'There is none,' The Functionary says. 'Not in your sense. There is only the creation of new apparatuses. To escape this box, you must build a new one. But the new box will have its own walls.'

'Then we are always trapped.'

'We are always playing,' The Functionary corrects. 'The difference between you and me is that you believe you are free. I know we are playing. Knowing lets me ask: who designed this game? Who benefits from this turn?'

The Pilot flies on. The clouds are beautiful. The wind is steady. He is still a master. But his hands feel heavier on the yoke. Every bank, every climb, every perfect response to the weather—all pre-written in the grammar of the machine.

'I do not like your philosophy,' The Pilot says.

'Nobody does,' The Functionary replies, taking another photograph. *Click.* 'That is why they call it freedom.'
